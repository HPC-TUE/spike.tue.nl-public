Access to Spike begins with a project discussion, not an account checklist. The
SCC uses the intake to understand whether Spike is the right platform, whether
the project is ready to use it, and whether capacity is available during the
requested period.

## Submit the request

Apply through either:

- The [TU/e Service Desk form](https://tue.topdesk.net/tas/public/ssp/content/serviceflow?unid=93e8b8b00471499593597171aaddaf34);
- The [Research Cockpit](https://placeholder_research_cockpit_url/).

A useful request gives the AI engineer enough context for a productive first
conversation. Include:

- The research goal and the TU/e department or institute involved;
- A proposed project name;
- The people responsible for the project and the full list of users who will
  need access;
- What the code does today, where it currently runs, and how close it is to a
  meaningful HPC test;
- The expected number and type of GPUs, run duration, and rough project window;
- The estimated dataset size, its current location, whether it is ready to
  transfer, and its expected sensitivity classification;
- The software stack and whether a working container already exists;
- Why Spike may be a better fit than the resources you already use.

Exact numbers are not required at this stage. A reasoned estimate is more useful
than an ambitious guess.

## Projects with external collaborators

External collaborators can be included in a Spike project, but they need an
approved TU/e sponsored or guest identity before SCC can grant Run:ai and
Harbor access. Mention external users in the initial request so the identity
process can start before onboarding; it can take longer than adding an existing
TU/e account.

Provide SCC with the collaborator's name, home institution, role in the
project, TU/e project sponsor, and expected access period. Do not send passport
copies, dates of birth, nationality details, or other identity documents to SCC
or attach them to a Spike ticket. TU/e HR and identity-management services
collect any information they require through their own approved process. SCC
continues onboarding after the institutional identity and project approval are
confirmed.

## Is Spike the right fit?

Spike is TU/e's most capable GPU platform. It is intended for work that benefits
from capabilities such as:

- Distributed training across several GPUs or nodes;
- Unusually large GPU-memory requirements;
- Blackwell-specific features such as driver-level FP4 precision;
- The NVIDIA AI Enterprise software stack;
- GPU Direct Storage and high-throughput data pipelines;
- Container-based research workflows;
- Runs that exceed the practical capacity or time limits of other available
  platforms.

A project does not need to meet every condition. The intake is where SCC and the
research team decide whether the expected benefit justifies using scarce Spike
capacity. A workload that runs comfortably on one conventional GPU may be
better served elsewhere.

For the current institutional criteria, see
[Is Spike-1 the right fit for your project?](https://supercomputing.tue.nl/spike-1/#is-spike-1-the-right-fit-for-your-project).

## The intake conversation

An SCC AI engineer will discuss the research objective, technical readiness,
data, software environment, required scale, and project schedule with you. The
conversation is collaborative: its purpose is to find the right computing route
and to make sure a Spike allocation can be used well.

The outcome is one of three practical next steps:

1. **Onboarding is scheduled** when the project is eligible, ready, and capacity
   is available.
2. **The project joins the waiting list** when it is a good fit but the cluster
   is full during the requested period.
3. **Another route is recommended** when
   [Snellius](https://www.surf.nl/en/services/compute/snellius-the-national-supercomputer),
   the [TU/e Umbrella cluster](https://supercomputing.tue.nl/), or
   [SURF Research Cloud](https://www.surf.nl/en/services/compute/surf-research-cloud)
   better matches the workload, or when the project needs more preparation
   first.

## Prepare for onboarding

Onboarding takes part of a day and is a working session with an AI engineer.
Come with:

- Code you understand and can run in a known environment, close enough to HPC
  readiness that a meaningful test does not depend on weeks of further
  development;
- The dataset you plan to use, ready to transfer, with its size and sensitivity
  classification understood;
- The agreed project name and complete list of users;
- An estimate of the full dataset size and a confirmed sensitivity
  classification;
- The command or entry point for a small test run;
- A list of required packages, system libraries, and accelerator dependencies;
- A plan for durable input, output, and long-term storage;
- Enough Linux and terminal experience to diagnose your own application.

Together you will cover the Spike-specific parts: project access, Harbor and
containers, storage and data transfer, Run:ai environments, resource selection,
workload submission, logs, and cleanup. Some adjustment to containers and the
B200 GPUs is expected; that is part of the onboarding work.

## Data and access window

Spike access is granted for a defined project window. Plan how results and other
important data will leave the platform before that window closes.

Also account for Spike's quarterly maintenance period. The system is unavailable
for approximately one to two weeks, with dates planned and communicated in
advance together with active users.

Do not bring non-anonymized or non-pseudonymized personal data, direct end-user
data processing, or data above the permitted sensitivity level to Spike. Confirm
the classification and handling plan with your data steward before onboarding.

## Support after onboarding

The handover does not end when an account works. An SCC AI engineer remains the
technical contact while the team starts its first workloads. Use that contact
to review resource choices, container and data-flow problems, failed or slow
runs, and opportunities to use the system more efficiently.
